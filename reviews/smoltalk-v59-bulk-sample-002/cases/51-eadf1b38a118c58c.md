### Turn 1 - USER

<pre>
I am a developer that has been employed at the same company for 7 years. Our team has developed and is maintaining 5 or so different applications in various states of maturity. Four of the applications are built in Java (using Spring) and the other is built using .NET. My problem is that while my skills are up to date with Java I feel my skills in .NET are behind. I&#x27;m considering taking a course but I&#x27;m not sure if it would be worth my investment.
</pre>

### Turn 2 - ASSISTANT

<pre>
It&#x27;s natural to feel uncertain about investing time and resources in upskilling, especially when it&#x27;s not immediately clear if it will bring tangible benefits to your current role. Let&#x27;s break this down. You&#x27;ve been with the same company for 7 years, which suggests stability and possibly a sense of security. However, having a single application in .NET might indicate that it&#x27;s a specific, niche need within your organization.

Consider a few questions: Are there any plans or indications that the .NET application will be growing in complexity or importance within the company? Are there other team members with .NET expertise who could potentially leave or take on different responsibilities, making your skills more critical? Lastly, how much of your current workload involves the .NET application, and do you see that changing?

Taking a course to update your .NET skills might be beneficial if you can envision scenarios where having those skills would significantly impact your work or position within the company. On the other hand, if the .NET application is relatively stable and not expected to grow or change significantly, it might not be the best use of your time.

It&#x27;s also worth considering whether there are other areas you could invest in that might have a broader impact on your career. For example, learning more about cloud computing, DevOps practices, or even expanding your Java skills further could potentially open up more opportunities both within your current company and beyond.

Ultimately, it&#x27;s about weighing the potential benefits against the cost of your time and resources. If you&#x27;re still unsure, you might consider discussing your career development and the company&#x27;s technology roadmap with your manager or a mentor to get a clearer picture of where your efforts would be best spent.
</pre>

### Turn 4 - ASSISTANT

<pre>
Determining the future plans for the .NET application can be as simple as having a conversation with the right people within your organization. You might start by talking to your immediate supervisor or the product owner for the .NET application to understand if there are any upcoming initiatives, enhancements, or changes in the business that could impact the application.

You could also reach out to colleagues who work closely with the stakeholders or end-users of the .NET application. Sometimes, those who are more customer-facing may have insights into upcoming needs or pain points that could drive changes to the application.

Additionally, consider attending meetings or reading up on company-wide updates that discuss strategic goals and technology roadmaps. These sources can provide clues about where the company is investing its resources and whether the .NET application might be impacted.

Some specific questions to ask during these conversations include: Are there any significant updates or features planned for the .NET application in the next 6-12 months? Are there changes in the business or market that could lead to increased usage or reliance on the .NET application? Are there any plans to retire or replace the .NET application with a different solution?

By gathering this information, you can better understand the potential trajectory of the .NET application and make a more informed decision about whether investing in .NET skills aligns with your career goals and the company&#x27;s needs.
