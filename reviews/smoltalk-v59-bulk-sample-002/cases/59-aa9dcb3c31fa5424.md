In Abstract Algebra, homomorphisms are structure-preserving functions between algebraic objects, and in this case, we&#x27;re looking for homomorphisms from the group of integers, ℤ, to itself.

Since ℤ is an additive group, we&#x27;re seeking functions φ: ℤ → ℤ that satisfy the homomorphism property: φ(a + b) = φ(a) + φ(b) for all a, b in ℤ.

To determine these homomorphisms, consider that any homomorphism φ must map the identity element of ℤ (which is 0) to itself, since φ(0 + 0) = φ(0) + φ(0) implies φ(0) = 0.

Next, observe that for any integer n, φ(n) = φ(1 + ... + 1) = nφ(1), where the sum has n terms. This means that φ is completely determined by the value it assigns to 1.

Now, let&#x27;s examine the possible values for φ(1). Since φ is a homomorphism, it must preserve the additive structure of ℤ. If φ(1) = k for some integer k, then φ(n) = kn. This is indeed a homomorphism, since φ(a + b) = k(a + b) = ka + kb = φ(a) + φ(b).

Therefore, the homomorphisms from ℤ to ℤ are precisely the functions of the form φ(n) = kn, where k is an integer. These are often referred to as the &quot;multiplication by k&quot; homomorphisms.

To solve the differential equation dy/dx = -x/y using separation of variables, we&#x27;ll rearrange the equation to isolate the variables x and y on opposite sides of the equation.

First, multiply both sides by y to get: y(dy/dx) = -x. Then, multiply by dx to get: y dy = -x dx.

Now we&#x27;ve separated the variables, with all instances of y on the left-hand side and all instances of x on the right-hand side.

Next, integrate both sides of the equation. The left-hand side becomes ∫y dy = (1/2)y^2, and the right-hand side becomes ∫(-x) dx = -(1/2)x^2.

So, we now have (1/2)y^2 = -(1/2)x^2 + C, where C is the constant of integration.

Multiplying both sides by 2 and rearranging, we obtain y^2 + x^2 = 2C.

If we let 2C = k, where k is a constant, we can rewrite the solution as x^2 + y^2 = k. This is the general solution to the differential equation.

This solution represents a family of concentric circles centered at the origin (0, 0) in the x-y plane, with the radius of each circle determined by the constant k.

Note that this differential equation is a simple example of an exact differential equation that models certain types of conservative systems, where the total energy (represented by the sum of the squares of x and y) remains constant.

Let&#x27;s start by considering the equation of a circle in rectangular coordinates. If the circle is centered at the origin (0, 0) and has radius r, its equation is given by x^2 + y^2 = r^2. This is because the distance from any point (x, y) on the circle to the origin is given by the radius r, and the Pythagorean theorem implies that the sum of the squares of the coordinates is equal to the square of the distance.

Now, let&#x27;s consider the same circle using complex variables. In the complex plane, we can represent a point (x, y) as a complex number z = x + iy, where i is the imaginary unit (satisfying i^2 = -1). The equation of the circle can then be written as |z| = r, where |z| denotes the modulus (or magnitude) of the complex number z. The modulus of a complex number is defined as the distance from the origin to the point representing the number in the complex plane.

Recall that |z| = √(x^2 + y^2) for z = x + iy. So, the equation |z| = r is equivalent to √(x^2 + y^2) = r, which in turn is equivalent to x^2 + y^2 = r^2, the equation of the circle in rectangular coordinates.

Finally, let&#x27;s consider the equation of the circle using polar coordinates. In polar coordinates, a point (x, y) is represented by its distance r from the origin and its angle θ from the positive x-axis. We can write z = r eiθ, where e^(iθ) = cos(θ) + i sin(θ) is the complex exponential function. In polar coordinates, the equation of the circle is simply r = constant, where the constant is the radius of the circle. The angle θ is unrestricted, meaning it can take on any value from 0 to 2π (or any other full rotation).

So, to summarize: the equation of a circle centered at the origin in the complex plane is x^2 + y^2 = r^2 in rectangular coordinates, |z| = r in complex variables, and r = constant in polar coordinates.
